# Problem brief

**Every Vote, Every Voice · Team 8 · Tallie**

## The link we chose: collation

A Nigerian election is a chain. Voters are registered, materials are moved, people vote, ballots are counted in public at 176,846 polling units, and each unit's result sheet is photographed to INEC's public portal. Then the figures are added up on paper: at about 8,800 ward centres, 774 local governments, 37 states, and nationally. A returning officer declares the winner.

We chose the adding up.

## Why it matters

- **It is where results have been shown to change.** In Rivers State in 2023 the BBC counted the published polling-unit sheets and found the declared figure for the winning candidate about 106,000 higher than the sheets. In Edo in 2024, observers' estimates fell outside the declared results in three local governments. In both cases the polling-unit count was public; the change came afterwards.
- **It is the part nobody can see.** INEC publishes polling-unit sheets. It does not publish ward or local-government collation forms, and said in November 2026 that it will not for 2027.
- **The public half is already published.** Anyone could add up the polling-unit sheets. Almost nobody can, because it is 176,846 photographs.
- **Doubt spreads faster than checking.** On election night a voter hears that results were changed, that a portal was hacked, that voting was extended. They have no quick way to see what has actually been reported and by whom.

## Who it affects

- **Voters**, especially first-time voters, who photograph the sheet on the wall and later wonder whether that is what was counted.
- **Journalists and observers**, who need to know which of 774 local governments to look at first.
- **Party agents and candidates**, who have 21 days to file a petition and must tie evidence to specific polling units.
- **INEC itself**, whose results are doubted even where they are sound, because nobody can check.

## What Tallie does about it

1. **Follows the count.** It adds up the published polling-unit results for every ward, local government and state, and sets each sum beside the announced figure. It flags a mismatch only when the sheets still missing could not account for it.
2. **Researches what was reported.** For a flagged mismatch, or any rumour a voter sends, an agent searches news, observer statements and social media, and returns a short note with its sources and how solid the evidence is.
3. **Starts where the voter is.** The same companion tells a voter whether their card is ready, whether their polling unit moved, and what other voters at that unit report, then leads from their unit to their ward's count.

It works as an app, and over WhatsApp and SMS, in English and Pidgin, with research notes also in Hausa, Yorùbá and Igbo.

## What it does not do

Tallie never declares a result or names a winner. It shows a sum of INEC's own published figures, says how many sheets it covers, and describes differences without accusing anyone. It detects and documents; it does not prevent.

## Proof it works

Run on real data from Rivers State in 2023, Tallie flags Obio/Akpor and Oyigbo, the two local governments the BBC reported, and leaves the other 21 unflagged.
